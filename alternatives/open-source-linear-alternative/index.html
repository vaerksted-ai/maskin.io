Open source Linear alternative: the closed-loop bet workspace

The open source Linear alternative most teams actually want isn't a faster ticket list — it's a workspace where the work closes. If you've searched "open source Linear alternative," you're usually after two things: escaping the per-seat bill and closed AI layer Linear locked in through 2026, and getting a tool your team can run on their own terms — self-hosted, model of your choice, agents you can inspect. The open-source options that fill this SERP — Plane, Focalboard, and OpenProject — solve the first part cleanly. They are self-hosted issue trackers: essentially the same issue-and-project object model Linear popularized, minus the seat cost. Maskin answers the second part differently. It's an Apache-2.0, MCP-native workspace where the unit of work is a bet — a testable hypothesis that runs from raw signal to a shipped, validated outcome, jointly owned by humans and agents. That closed loop is what separates it both from the OSS trackers and from Linear's own agent layer.

The Linear pain that actually sends people looking

Linear built its reputation on speed and taste — an opinionated issue tracker for teams that resent Jira. That is a genuine product achievement, and for anyone who just needs an efficient issue queue, Linear is a serious tool. Two things pushed teams to start searching for an alternative through 2026, though.

First, the per-seat bill compounds. Linear's model scales linearly with the team you actually want to include — engineering, design, PM, sometimes support and marketing — and the moment agents enter the picture that pricing shape starts to look strange, because "assign work to an agent" is not a category the seat model was built to price.

Second, the AI layer is closed. Linear Agent shipped in March 2026, grounded in issues, roadmap, and code (Linear changelog, March 2026). It works. But you don't choose the model, you can't inspect the loop, and you can't run it inside your own boundary. For teams that adopted agent-native workflows in 2026 specifically because they wanted more control over what the AI does, not less, that's the wrong direction.

An open source alternative is what "escape both" looks like when written as a search query. But the search hides a fork most people don't see until they've clicked five results: whether you want the same shape of tool under a different license, or a different shape of work entirely.

Why "open source alone" isn't a strategy

The alternatives that currently rank for "open source Linear alternative" — Plane, Focalboard, and OpenProject — are earnest, self-hostable trackers. Their appeal is real: no per-seat pricing, your data on your own server, and a codebase you can audit. But they measure themselves against Linear on Linear's own axis: how efficient the issue queue is. The unit of work underneath is still an issue and a project — and none of them runs the closed-loop bet structure that is the actual case for switching away from what Linear does.

Open source is a license, not a product decision. A permissively licensed clone of an issue queue reproduces an issue queue's ceiling. The right question isn't "can I self-host it?" — it's "what unit of work does the tool actually run on?"

That question is where the real fork sits, and it's the reason Maskin appears on this search at all.

The incumbents — and the OSS newcomers — all moved toward agents on top of tickets

Linear was one of the first to formalise the pattern. Linear Agent (March 2026) grounds an agent in the roadmap, issues, and code (Linear changelog, March 2026). Jira did the same via Rovo and Atlassian's Teamwork Graph, reporting agentic automations up ~7× in six months across the enterprise cloud base (Atlassian, Team '26, May 2026). Notion shipped Custom Agents in February 2026 and a Developer Platform for cross-tool agents in May 2026 (Notion via TechCrunch, May 2026).

Through 2026, the same pattern spread to a new wave of Apache- and AGPL-licensed AI-native trackers making the same shared claims: MCP-native, self-hostable, agents as first-class members. That crowding matters here, because it means MCP-native + self-host + agents-as-teammates are now table stakes on this SERP. Anyone leading with only those claims is repeating the field.

Watch what none of them changed: the object of work is still an issue, a sprint, or a spec document. The AI reads history and drafts against a backlog; it doesn't carry a decision from insight to validated outcome. That closed loop is the open space.

The honest cut: a bet object, not an issue

Linear models a fast backlog — issues in projects, cycles, and priorities. Plane and Focalboard model a slightly different flavour of the same object. Maskin models a learning loop, and the difference is structural, not cosmetic:

  1. Signal arrives. Feedback, churn risk, market moves, and competitive shifts land continuously from Slack, Intercom, PostHog, HubSpot, and GitHub.
  2. Raw signal becomes a bet. A testable hypothesis with a clear win condition, shaped from signal and jointly owned by a human and agents.
  3. The work runs. Tasks execute — by humans and agents — each with the context it needs.
  4. It ships and validates. When the bet closes, the outcome is measured against the win condition and the people who asked are notified.
  5. The loop rules. Validated bets inform the next set. The product of work isn't an issue that closes in a project; it's a decision that earns its keep.

This is the closed-loop pattern the industry now calls loop engineering — expressed in Maskin as first-class workspace objects a team configures, not code a developer wires. Agents run on triggers and events rather than sitting in a chat box next to a board, because the loop is bounded by close conditions and human promote gates, not open-ended back-and-forth (Maskin architecture, 2026). For the full doctrine behind planning in bets rather than backlogs, see the companion piece on bet-based product planning.

The choice among them comes down to whether you want to run the same shape of work more cheaply, or change the shape of the work itself. If the team's job is "triage, track, and close issues faster than Linear does," you don't need a closed loop — you need a better issue tracker, and one of the OSS options above is probably the answer. If the job is "turn signal into shipped, validated outcomes with agents," that's the loop no issue model expresses. That shape of work — where agents and humans share a typed object model — is what we call an AI-native agentic workspace.

For teams already comparing options as an AI product management tool and asking where the loop actually sits in the stack, this is the honest split.

Where the AI layer actually sits — and why it matters for data

Linear's agent, and the AI layers bolted onto the OSS trackers, read history and produce output. In Maskin, agents are part of the loop, which changes where your data flows in a material way:

  • Triggers bring signal in — community, CRM, support, GitHub, and competitor streams — on a schedule, not when someone remembers to check.
  • Marketplace loops wire the agents they need — customer feedback, market signals, churn-and-expansion, lost-deals-to-roadmap, competitive intelligence.
  • Human judgement gates decisions. Agents bring the signal; humans bring the judgement. The loop stays bounded, and people are pulled in only at the taste points.
  • Validation is built in. When something ships, the outcome is measured and the requester is notified.

Because the unit of work is a bet with a condition to measure against, auditability means something concrete: you can see what a bet claimed, what it shipped, and whether the claim held. That's a stronger answer to "what did the AI actually do?" than a changelog next to an issue.

Who should switch — and who shouldn't

Switch to Maskin if the search that brought you here was really "escape Linear's per-seat model and closed AI layer, and stop pretending the job is issue triage." If you collect signal from customers and the market but it drowns in inboxes and abandoned issues, and you want an AI tool that doesn't just summarise but does the work — under a licence you control — that's the fit.

Keep Linear, Plane, Focalboard, or OpenProject if you genuinely only need a high-efficiency issue queue and the work already fits comfortably into that shape. Maskin isn't an issue queue, and it won't turn itself into one to win the comparison.

If your "alternative" search is really about the agent platform rather than the tracker — Claude Cowork, Codex, or a hosted agent workspace — that's a different question, and the open source alternative to Claude Cowork page covers it directly.

FAQ

Can Maskin replace Linear?

Not as a drop-in issue tracker. It uses a different object model — bets and loops instead of issues and cycles — and it integrates with the tools you already run rather than asking you to move everything in. If you need an issue queue, keep Linear. If your pain with Linear is per-seat cost plus a closed AI layer, replacing it with an AI-native agentic workspace sitting alongside your existing tools will do more than swapping to another queue.

Why isn't Plane, Focalboard, or OpenProject enough?

Those are solid open-source issue trackers that solve cost and self-hosting around the issue model. What they don't solve is the loop: none turns raw signal into a testable bet that a human and an agent own together, and none validates the outcome against a win criterion automatically.

How is this different from Linear Agent?

Linear Agent works well as an AI layer on top of Linear's issue model — it drafts, summarises, and executes against the roadmap. It stays inside Linear's environment, on Linear's models, and closed to inspection. Maskin's loop runs on a typed object model you can read, self-host, and point at the model of your choice. Same family of capability; a fundamentally different relationship to your data and your model choice.

Does open source really matter for an AI tool?

More than for normal SaaS. The tool decides which agents see what, how loops are bounded, and what happens to your data. Apache 2.0 means you can read the behaviour, modify it, and run it commercially — a permissive licence, not a look-but-don't-touch one.

What does it cost to self-host?

Nothing for the software — self-host is always free. You pay only for your own infrastructure and model usage.

Can I try it before committing?

Yes. Self-host is always free, and there is a hosted option if you'd rather evaluate without running infrastructure. See maskin.io for current plans.

Is Maskin production-hardened?

Not yet, and it would be dishonest to claim otherwise. Maskin is an early-stage open-source project with a strong architectural position: Apache 2.0, self-hosting, model choice, and a closed loop on a unified object model. If your evaluation criterion is a proven, mature vendor track record, weigh that against your appetite for shaping the category.
