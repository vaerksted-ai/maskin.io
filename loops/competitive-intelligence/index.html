Competitive intelligence loop: two lanes, one signal, both close

A competitive intelligence loop watches competitor changelogs, pricing pages, GitHub releases, and Product Hunt launches and routes every signal into two lanes at once: a draft battlecard update for sales, and a shaped bet for product where the signal reveals a real gap. Sales enablement approves the battlecard, a PM approves the bet, and both lanes close: the battlecard ships to the sales channel, the bet's outcome is measured. The wedge against Klue, Crayon, and Kompyte is not the classifier; it is that both lanes close instead of one lane ending on a dashboard.

What a competitive intelligence loop actually is

A competitive intelligence loop is a closed cycle that watches structured competitor signal (changelogs, pricing pages, GitHub release notes, Product Hunt launches) and routes each signal into two lanes at once. On one lane, sales gets a draft battlecard update so the field response is prepared before the next call. On the other, product gets a shaped bet where the signal reveals a real gap, with a win condition attached. Both lanes close in the tools their owners already live in. It is the kind of closed loop an agentic workspace runs: the one that keeps sales and product aligned on what a competitor just did.

The honest read on competitive intelligence software today is that everyone can watch competitors. Klue, Crayon, Kompyte, and the wave of newer AI-native scanners all cover the capture layer competently. The gap is not detection. The gap is routing: most CI tools produce one lane, typically a battlecard update in the sales enablement platform, and the same signal never reaches product with commitment attached. When a competitor ships a feature that means both "we lose deals when this comes up" and "we have a real product gap," a one-lane tool answers half the question. This loop is built to answer both.

The four moments that make it a loop

Signal is captured across the four sources. Connectors monitor competitor changelogs, pricing pages, GitHub releases, and Product Hunt launches. Not every signal source is equal: pricing pages are noisy and load-bearing; GitHub releases are cheap to watch and rarely surprising; Product Hunt is high-signal for early competitors and low for established ones. The capture step is deliberately narrow: changes to the page, not scrapes of the marketing copy.

Signal is classified into one or both lanes. An agent looks at the captured change and asks two independent questions: does this need a battlecard update (does it change how a rep should handle a competitive deal), and does it reveal a product gap (does it change what we should build). The two questions are not exclusive. A changelog entry can be both a battlecard candidate and a gap candidate, and the loop tags it both ways rather than picking one. This is the load-bearing detail: forcing every signal into one lane loses half the information every time.

Two-lane routing produces two prepared decisions. For the battlecard lane, an agent drafts the update (the new claim, the counter, the objection handling) and posts it to sales enablement for approval. For the gap lane, an agent shapes a bet with a win condition of the form "ship response X, close-rate against competitor Y improves by Z pp on the affected segment." Sales enablement approves the battlecard; a PM approves the bet. The taste gates stay human on each lane, which is where they should be: sales enablement knows if the battlecard is honest; the PM knows if the bet is the right shape of work.

Both lanes close. The battlecard update ships to the sales channel the field actually reads. The bet, once shipped, has its win condition measured against competitor close-rate movement on the affected segment. The loop closes on both outcomes. Neither lane is allowed to end on a dashboard. That is the failure mode the loop was built to prevent.

Why two-lane routing beats a one-lane CI dashboard

A one-lane CI tool has an internally consistent story: the buyer is sales enablement, the deliverable is a battlecard, and the tool ships. It is a good story if you only care about the sales lane. Where it breaks is when the same competitor signal is also a product gap, which, for anything more consequential than a copy tweak, it usually is. The product-facing signal is left on the floor because the tool has no obligation to close it, and no obligation to shape it into work.

A two-lane loop does not try to be a better classifier than the incumbents. Klue and Crayon can produce excellent battlecards. The wedge is structural: the loop has an equal obligation to both lanes, and it closes both. A battlecard that ships and a bet whose outcome is measured are two closed loops from one signal. A battlecard alone is one closed loop and one silently dropped one. Over the course of a year, the dropped lane is what compounds into a product that is systematically one competitor behind on features and prices.

The object model that makes the loop automatable

A loop that owns two lanes at once needs the underlying work to be machine-readable on both sides. Maskin runs the loop on typed objects in a unified model (competitor pages, captured signals, battlecard drafts, bets, and tasks are all objects connected by typed relationships), driven by triggers that fire an agent when the relevant object changes state.

Concretely: a scheduled trigger polls the four sources (changelog, pricing page, GitHub, Product Hunt) and captures diffs onto signal objects; an agent classifies each signal into the battlecard lane, the gap lane, or both; a drafting agent produces a battlecard update on the sales lane and a shaped bet on the product lane; sales enablement approves the battlecard from Slack or the enablement tool; a PM approves the bet in the workspace; execution agents ship both; validation compares post-ship close-rate movement to the bet's win condition and confirms the battlecard reached the sales channel. Connectors to Slack, HubSpot, Salesforce, GitHub, PostHog, and the enablement platform carry both lanes end to end.

On the product lane, the shaped bet lands as a first-class object in an AI product management tool, not a ticket in a backlog, not a note in a doc, with a driver, a win condition, and lineage back to the competitor signal that produced it. That is what lets the gap lane behave like any other bet on the roadmap: it competes for cycle capacity on the same terms, it ships with the same taste gate, and its outcome is measured against a real win condition rather than a vague sense that the team "responded" to a competitor.

The two-lane routing lives on the object model rather than in prompt scaffolding, which is what lets both lanes close independently. A signal is not routed to one lane and lost from the other; it is tagged for both lanes on the same object, and each lane has its own owner and its own close condition. This is the same division Maskin's other loops use: agents run the mechanical work, humans keep the taste, and the object graph carries the state so nothing gets silently dropped.

The honest limits of competitive intelligence

Competitor signal is real, and the loop is honest about its constraints. Marketing copy is not evidence of a product change. Pricing pages are updated for A/B tests as often as for real pricing moves. A GitHub release note might reflect a two-line bug fix that reads like a feature launch. Any classifier, including a good one, will occasionally send a nothing-burger into both lanes, and the human gates are how the loop stays useful past month one.

The loop does not compete on classification accuracy. It competes on the fact that both lanes close, and that classification errors are cheap to reject because sales enablement or the PM sees the drafted output before it ships. The category around this is crowded. CI tools with polished dashboards are not going away, and the honest position of the loop is not "our classifier is better than Klue's" but "our routing is two lanes instead of one, and both close." That is a smaller claim, and a more defensible one, than a claim about better detection.

FAQ

What is a competitive intelligence loop?

A competitive intelligence loop is a closed cycle that watches competitor signal (changelogs, pricing pages, GitHub releases, Product Hunt launches) and routes each signal into two lanes at once: a draft battlecard update for sales and a shaped bet for product. Sales enablement approves the battlecard; a PM approves the bet; both lanes close when the battlecard ships and the bet's outcome is measured. It is the difference between watching competitors and closing the loop on what they do next.

How is this different from Klue, Crayon, or Kompyte?

Those tools own the sales lane of CI competently. The gap is that the same signal is often a product gap too, and a one-lane CI tool routes it to sales and stops. This loop routes to both lanes at once and closes both. It does not compete on classifier accuracy (Klue can produce excellent battlecards); it competes on the fact that the product-facing signal reaches product as a shaped bet with a win condition instead of dying in an enablement dashboard.

Which competitor sources should the loop watch?

Start narrow with the sources where a diff is a real change: competitor changelogs, pricing pages, GitHub release notes, and Product Hunt launches for early-stage competitors. Marketing copy is worth watching but should be treated as low-weight signal. The loop's classifier is only as good as the input; adding noisier sources without adjusting the human gates is how a CI loop becomes a firehose the sales team learns to ignore.

Do we still need humans in the loop?

Yes, on both lanes, and the loop is built around it. Sales enablement approves the battlecard update: they know whether the drafted claim is honest and whether the objection handling is what a rep can actually use. A PM approves the bet: they know whether the gap is a roadmap problem or a marketing problem, and whether the win condition is right. Removing either human gate is how one lane ships junk and the other lane silently drops. The gates are the loop's quality control, not its bottleneck.

What happens when a signal is only one lane, not both?

Most signals are one-lane, and the loop is fine with that. A minor copy tweak is a battlecard update and not a product gap; a deep architectural change might be a real gap without needing a battlecard update. The classifier tags one lane, the other is left empty, and the loop still closes on the lane that fired. The two-lane structure is what catches the both-lane signals; it does not force every signal to be both.
